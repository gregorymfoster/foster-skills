# Market lenses

Five lenses about the world outside the team: the problem, the money, the competition, the timing, and the comparables. Each gets a verdict (Pass, Mostly pass, Likely pass, Partial pass, Fail, Unknown) and a specific why. Gather evidence before judging; link every number.

## 1. Problem and buyer urgency

Who has the problem, how often, and what it costs them when it bites. Urgency is high when the buyer has a deadline, a regulator, a lost deal, or a recent incident; low when the problem is a mild annoyance they've lived with for years.

- Name the buyer (the person who signs) and the user (the person who touches it daily). They're often different.
- Look for what they do about it today. A spreadsheet, a contractor, a script, or a manual ritual is a good sign: they already spend time or money. "Nothing" is usually a warning.
- Check the public evidence against the founders' framing. Surveys and forum threads often show the real pain is different from the pitch (for example, buyers who already use a tool unsafely, rather than buyers waiting to adopt one). Say how the evidence changes the pitch.

Verdict scale for the scorecard: High, Medium, Low.

## 2. Market size

Estimate it three ways and show the arithmetic, so a reader can check it:

1. **Top-down.** The category's spend from analyst or public figures, then the slice this idea can serve.
2. **Bottom-up.** Number of target customers times a realistic annual price. Example: 40,000 US firms in the segment times $12,000 a year is about $480M.
3. **Adjacent spend.** What buyers already pay for neighboring products (a leader's ARR, a category's revenue). This is often the most honest proof that the layer is worth money.

Then the **1% check**: what does 1% of the bottom-up market come to in annual revenue? If 1% is under about $10M, the idea is a lifestyle business at best unless the market grows or the scope widens. Say whether that matters given the team's funding preference.

Name the trap when there is one: the free or seed segment that spends little while the money sits with a different buyer (compliance teams, enterprises), open-source infrastructure that is hard to charge for, services revenue that only grows with headcount, or a market whose budgets are small (many consumer and education markets). Markets the founder profile lists under "markets to avoid" fail here.

## 3. Competitors and crowding

Build a table of who's already there: name, what they sell, size or funding (round, amount, lead, date), and who they sell to. Include platform owners who could bundle it free. Then judge three things.

**Crowded but mediocre.** Split crowded into two kinds. Crowded with weak, unbacked tools (one-person repos, half-finished extensions, nothing polished or backed for the long term) is open, and is often the best opening because the need is proven. Crowded with polished, well-funded leaders is closed for a horizontal product. If the field is split (polished at the enterprise end, nothing for small companies), say which slice the idea targets and whether that slice is open.

**The internal-tool test.** Does a version already exist, hacked together inside a top company, that nobody has made generic and easy to adopt? Pass when you can name the internal tool with a source (an engineering blog, a talk, an open-sourced repo, press with usage numbers). Many durable companies are one firm's in-house system repackaged for everyone (Airbnb's StreamAlert became Panther, Uber's Michelangelo became Tecton, Facebook's osquery led to Kolide and Fleet; from memory, not re-checked). If it's already been productized well, the test passes on the first half and crowding likely fails.

**The wedge.** In a crowded market, better execution is not enough. Name the wedge the leaders are structurally unable or unwilling to chase: a segment too small for their sales team, a deployment model that breaks their business (self-hosted against their cloud), a price point that would cannibalize them, a workflow they don't own. If there's no such wedge, crowding fails.

Also give **platform risk** (Low, Medium, High, Very high): how likely a platform owner (an OS, a cloud, a suite, a model lab) gives the same thing away within 18 months. Look at their release notes and roadmaps, not guesses.

Watch for the **empty-for-a-reason** case. A market with almost nothing funded may be an opening or a tarpit: a problem many smart teams have tried for a decade without anyone paying. Name the earlier attempts and why they failed before calling an empty market open.

## 4. Why now

Name the specific change, with a date and a source: a platform shift, a vendor tightening API terms, a regulation, a cost collapse, an acquisition that strands customers. Pass when the change is recent, concrete, and makes the idea possible or necessary today. Fail when the honest answer is "it was always a good idea". Also say when the window might close, for example when incumbents are visibly converging.

## 5. Comparables

Name two or three companies that won with a similar shape (go-to-market, buyer, business model) and one or two cautionary tales that tried something similar and failed or stalled. Say what each one teaches for this idea in a sentence. Comparables are for pattern-matching, not proof; mark any you didn't verify.
