# Business lenses

Four lenses about how the idea becomes a business: how it gets adopted, whether it can start free, how it makes money, and what protects it. Each gets a verdict and a specific why.

## 1. Adoption and distribution

Trace the path from a stranger to a paying customer, step by step, and find where it breaks.

- **First user.** Who tries it first, and can they do it alone? Note blockers: locked-down laptops, admin-only installs, procurement, security review, data-access approvals, hardware.
- **Time to value.** How long from first touch to the first moment it's clearly useful? Minutes is a strength; weeks of setup or data import is a risk that needs a concierge onboarding plan.
- **Spread.** Does one user pull in colleagues without being asked (shared artifacts, invites, the tool being visible in others' work)? Estimate organic installs per seeded user, and say what would make it spread.
- **Path to the buyer.** How does usage reach the person who signs? Intros from active users, an admin noticing, a compliance need.
- **Channels.** Where the first hundred customers come from: communities, search, marketplaces, partners or resellers, warm intros, outbound. For each, a reason to believe it works for this buyer. Buyers who don't post online (a dozen specialist teams, senior partners) mean direct sales only.
- **Switching cost.** What they give up to move from today's tool or habit, and what the idea does to lower it (import, coexist, a free migration).

Verdict: Pass when there's a believable path at every step; name the weakest step either way. Give the riskiest step its own assumption in the assumption map.

## 2. Free-first

Can it be given away to earn trust and usage, with users paying first in time, feedback, usage and introductions? Teams that prefer to sell paid contracts from day one can drop this lens in their founder profile. Five sub-tests, each yes, weak or no:

1. **Adopt without buying.** An individual can start without a purchase decision, procurement or IT approval. Hardware fails.
2. **Near-zero cost to serve.** Free users cost almost nothing (runs locally, or hosted cheaply). Heavy inference on the company's bill fails unless capped.
3. **Usage compounds.** Free use builds something more valuable over time: accumulated data, team habits, templates, network effects, public reputation.
4. **A natural paid layer, bought by someone else.** The team, firm or company pays for admin, policy, audit, hosting, retention, support or scale. If the only buyer is the same individual who got it free, the paid layer is weak.
5. **Beats the free options already out there.** This test knocks out more ideas than you'd expect.

Pass needs all five, or four with a clear plan for the fifth. Where buyers don't adopt bottoms-up, the free tier becomes a free firm pilot: still free-first, but slower. Pair every pass with a dated money test (around month 4) so free usage can't quietly become the whole strategy, and count usage as payment only with numbers: week-4 active installs, feedback calls taken, intros to the signer. In free-first, the vanity metric to fear is installs; track retention by install cohort.

## 3. Business model and unit economics

- **Who pays, for what, how much.** The pricing unit (seat, firm, usage, setup fee plus monthly) and a starting price anchored on what buyers pay today for neighboring things.
- **Time to first dollar.** Weeks or months until the first payment, assuming the team works the plan. Scorecard values: under 1 month, 1 to 2 months, 2 to 3 months, long.
- **Margins and delivery.** Software margins, or services-heavy early? Services are fine as a wedge if there's a path to product; say what it is.
- **Scale.** Venture scale (could plausibly reach $100M a year in revenue) or a business that pays for itself early and keeps the team default-alive. Neither is wrong; judge against the team's funding preference and time commitment. A side project benefits from an idea that pays for itself early.
- **Likely exit shape.** Standalone company, acquisition by an aggregator or platform, or a cash-flow business.

## 4. Moat and defensibility

What makes the idea harder to copy as it grows: data gravity (it becomes the system of record), network effects, workflow lock-in, integrations, brand and trust in a sensitive category, a supply side others can't easily build (an expert network, a regulated license), or speed in a niche the leaders ignore. Be honest when the only moat is execution, and say how fast a funded competitor could copy the first version. Note moats that erode (benchmarks and datasets that go stale, features a platform will absorb).
