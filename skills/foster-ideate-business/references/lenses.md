# Idea lenses

Each lens is a way of looking at the seeds that tends to turn up real ideas. Run all of them. For each idea, note which lens produced it and the proof of need (a company, a tool, a number, a thread). Two or more lenses agreeing on one idea is a strong sign.

## 1. The internal tool nobody made generic

Many durable companies are one firm's in-house system repackaged for everyone. A top company hacked it together because the need was real and nothing on the market fit; nobody has yet made it easy for ordinary companies to adopt. Examples from memory, not re-checked: Airbnb's StreamAlert became Panther, Uber's Michelangelo became Tecton, Facebook's osquery led to Kolide and Fleet.

Where to look: engineering blogs, conference talks, open-sourced repos with no company behind them, ex-employee posts that say "I miss our internal X", and the founders' own past jobs. For each find, ask whether a polished product already exists. If it does, the idea is probably taken; if only rough open-source code exists, it's a candidate.

## 2. Crowded with mediocre tools

A field full of weak, unbacked tools (one-person repos, half-finished extensions, aging products nobody loves) proves the need and leaves room for one polished, well-backed product. Search marketplaces, app stores and "best tools for X" threads, and read the one- and two-star reviews. Skip fields with polished, well-funded leaders unless there's a slice they ignore.

## 3. Free first, paid later

Look for products that one person could adopt for free and that grow something more valuable with use (data, habits, shared artifacts), with a paid layer that someone else buys (the team, the firm, compliance). Good starting points: a free tool that a paid product could grow around, an open-source project with heavy use and no company, and paid categories where a free version would beat the free options people already use.

## 4. The regional or market-specific version

A product that won in one market often has no good equivalent in another country, language, regulation or industry vertical. Start from category leaders and ask where they don't serve well: local payment rails, tax and labor rules, language, data-residency laws, an industry with its own workflow. Founders with a region, language or industry network score highest here. Check that the local market's budgets are big enough.

## 5. Founder-market fit first

Start from each founder's edges in the seeds and ask: what would a buyer who already trusts this person pay for? What did their past team buy, build or complain about? Which job do they know better than almost anyone? Ideas from this lens are often less flashy and more fundable.

## 6. The change that just happened

Look for something that changed in the last year or two: a platform shift, a vendor tightening API terms or prices, a new regulation with a date, a cost collapse, an acquisition that stranded customers, an incumbent sunsetting a product. For each change, ask who is newly in pain and who can now do something they couldn't. Date and source every change.

## 7. Services that should be software

Find work buyers pay agencies, contractors or offshore teams to do by hand, especially work that is repetitive and checkable. The first version can be a service that a founder runs, with software taking over step by step. This lens suits teams with a founder who can run a services operation.

## 8. The workaround

Search for spreadsheets, scripts, shared docs and manual rituals that teams use to plug a gap: job posts asking for someone to maintain one, template marketplaces, forum threads sharing them. A widely shared workaround is a spec for a product.
